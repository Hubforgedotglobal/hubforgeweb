# HubForge: a locally rooted, globally connected evidence ecosystem

**Research date:** 5 November 2026  
**Depth:** Deep desk research
**Sources cited:** 28  
**Planning horizon:** 2027–2036  
**Status:** Proposed strategic framework, not a validated causal model or adopted organisational plan.

## Executive summary

HubForge should organise its next decade around the conditions that allow people to generate, challenge and use evidence, rather than around the number of platforms it operates. Technical skills matter, but evidence use also depends on political demand, decision routines, organisational incentives and the legitimacy of the questions being asked. OECD's institutional analysis, an African evaluation study and research on commissioning each illuminate different parts of this problem; they do not demonstrate a single universal mechanism. [[1]](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/strengthening-national-evidence-informed-policymaking-ecosystems_855c5286-en/full-report/overview-of-lessons-learned-and-principles-for-healthy-evidence-informed-policymaking-ecosystems_f4089495.html) [[2]](https://aejonline.org/index.php/aej/article/view/792/1596) [[3]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1356389020976157)

The strategic opportunity is to connect practical support with changes in how evidence work is resourced and governed. Funders can finance evaluation while still leaving organisations without adequate time or indirect support. National organisations can gain authority without transferring it to communities. Local knowledge can enter a consultation without influencing a decision. These distinctions are supported by philanthropy research, West African localisation accounts and OECD's locally led development guidance. [[4]](https://www.geofunders.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/2025-National-Study-of-Philanthropic-Practice-Full-Report.pdf) [[5]](https://wacsi.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Localisation-Report-ENG-2.pdf) [[6]](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/practical-guidelines-for-supporting-locally-led-development_eaecf72b-en/full-report/action-area-locally-led-monitoring-evaluation-and-learning_8a830675.html)

Our recommendation is a portfolio of testable contributions: protect local learning capacity; strengthen community decision rights; widen fair routes into paid evaluation work; and steward accessible, accountable technology. Existing Playbooks, platforms, Eval Corps and advisory can contribute to those purposes. They cannot, by themselves, change procurement rules, institutional incentives or political priorities. The map should therefore show reinforcing traps, protective constraints and other actors' authority, not present HubForge at the centre of everything.

The proposed horizon is adaptive: validate and establish baselines in 2027–2029, replicate only where evidence and capacity justify it in 2030–2032, and seek durable institutional arrangements in 2033–2036. These are decision windows, not promised growth targets. A smaller, demonstrably useful portfolio is preferable to launching an initiative for every node on a map.

## Background and research boundaries

The unit of analysis is an evidence ecosystem: communities, implementing organisations, commissioners, evaluators, educational institutions, professional networks, public decision-makers, funders and technology stewards. They exchange money, work, information, legitimacy, data and authority. This framework distinguishes those exchanges from causal relationships between variables such as workload, decision influence and retained capability.

Systems maps are useful for exposing assumptions and discussing mechanisms, but neither a convincing drawing nor stakeholder agreement establishes causality. Methodological guidance recommends explicit boundaries, triangulation and validation; broader public-sector systems work similarly locates change in institutions rather than individual technical fixes. [[7]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9661310/) [[8]](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/systems-approaches-to-public-sector-challenges_9789264279865-en.html) A more recent triangulation paper is identified in the source register, but only its search summary was reviewed, so its methods are not treated as validated guidance here. [[9]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44260-024-00017-9)

This research preserves HubForge's global–local ambition. It does not assume identical conditions across countries, sectors, Indigenous communities or funding arrangements. Nor does it certify legal eligibility for grants or CSR. The historical UNDP baseline is useful for institutional questions but includes a 2015 survey and is not a current country ranking. [[10]](https://nec.undp.org/sites/default/files/2025-05/NEC_BaselineStudy.pdf)

## Key findings

### 1. More evidence does not automatically create more demand

The OECD review of European evidence-informed policymaking identifies weak demand, short-termism, organisational culture and brokerage as important barriers. An African study documents political and managerial pressures on evidence production and use in a specific Ugandan conservation context. Commissioning research examines how the process itself can constrain complexity-appropriate evaluation. Their scopes differ, but together they challenge the assumption that better methods alone resolve weak use. [[1]](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/strengthening-national-evidence-informed-policymaking-ecosystems_855c5286-en/full-report/overview-of-lessons-learned-and-principles-for-healthy-evidence-informed-policymaking-ecosystems_f4089495.html) [[2]](https://aejonline.org/index.php/aej/article/view/792/1596) [[3]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1356389020976157)

HubForge should test whether supported work reaches a named decision, not simply whether someone downloads a report. A dashboard may be technically successful and institutionally unused. An evaluation may influence a decision through a trusted broker rather than through a public launch. The strategic response is to identify decision owners, timing, incentives and contested questions before choosing a tool.

This is not an argument for serving decision-makers uncritically. Evidence can be used selectively or to legitimise a predetermined position. Independence, the visibility of disagreement and the ability to report uncomfortable findings belong in the model. The Ugandan study provides contextual evidence of such pressures, not a general accusation against governments or donors. [[2]](https://aejonline.org/index.php/aej/article/view/792/1596)

### 2. Funding arrangements shape capacity and accountability

The GEO 2025 survey concerns US grantmakers, not global CSR. It nevertheless supplies direct evidence that evaluation requirements can coexist with inadequate additional funding. OECD's localisation framework distinguishes funding quality and agency from the volume transferred. WACSI's qualitative research identifies both donor dependency and accountability weaknesses within local civil society. [[4]](https://www.geofunders.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/2025-National-Study-of-Philanthropic-Practice-Full-Report.pdf) [[11]](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pathways-towards-effective-locally-led-development-co-operation_51079bba-en/full-report/component-10.html) [[5]](https://wacsi.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Localisation-Report-ENG-2.pdf)

The resulting strategic hypothesis is a reporting trap: fragmented requirements increase workload; workload reduces protected learning time; weak local learning leaves less useful evidence for local decisions; external assurance demands then remain dominant. The research supports components, not that complete closed loop. HubForge should investigate it through staff time diaries, reporting inventories and joint conversations with commissioners, rather than claim it is already proven.

A practical contribution could be a small, jointly funded learning arrangement across several organisations: shared questions where appropriate, a common minimum reporting set, protected interpretation time and explicit responsibility for responding to findings. This should be piloted only with willing funders and implementing partners. It is a proposal, not an existing HubForge programme.

### 3. Local leadership means authority, not attendance

The 2026 OECD guidance supports locally defined success, local evaluation leadership and data governance. African Evaluation Principles emphasise mutual accountability and local knowledge creation. Community data-sovereignty guidance adds the normative principle that communities should control evaluation-generated knowledge; its implementation is not established by the guidance itself. [[6]](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/practical-guidelines-for-supporting-locally-led-development_eaecf72b-en/full-report/action-area-locally-led-monitoring-evaluation-and-learning_8a830675.html) [[12]](https://aejonline.org/index.php/aej/article/view/697/1257) [[13]](https://www.betterevaluation.org/ar/node/2698)

Participation should therefore be assessed through decisions: who can change the questions, refuse an extractive method, interpret contested findings, determine release conditions or require a response? A workshop headcount cannot answer those questions. Community representatives also do not necessarily speak for everyone. Gender, language, disability, age and marginalised identities can alter whose participation is safe or influential.

HubForge's contribution could include accessible question-setting and interpretation practices, community-facing feedback formats and negotiated data-use agreements. It should not assume it can represent communities or establish their priorities remotely. Compensated, locally facilitated validation is a necessary next step.

### 4. Career access requires changes on both sides of the market

The 2IEval practitioner account describes mentoring, professional networks and recognition alongside constraints in opportunity and language. A university-centre study reports qualitative learning benefits from applied evaluation work. Made in Africa Evaluation scholarship points to commissioning and methodological power, not simply insufficient training. [[14]](https://idev.afdb.org/sites/default/files/documents/files/IDEV_VOL%202_2024_ENG_Article2.pdf) [[15]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39167980) [[16]](https://aejonline.org/index.php/aej/article/view/627/1126)

The proposed career loop connects supervised practice, demonstrated capability and fair access to assignments. Its weak point is opportunity: greater skill does not guarantee a contract when selection depends on prior clients, geographic access or procurement criteria. Eval Corps may offer supervised contributions, but it should not be used to normalise replacing paid junior work with indefinite volunteering.

For HubForge, a useful test would follow participants beyond completion: whether they obtained paid assignments, received fair credit, gained decision-making responsibilities and continued in the field. Mentor workload and accessibility costs also matter. EvalYouth describes representation as part of its mission, while UNEG has produced a mapping of emerging-evaluator engagement; the latter was identified but not read in full, so no findings are inferred from it. [[17]](https://evalyouth.org/mission-strategy/) [[18]](https://unevaluation.org/download-zip/15522)

### 5. AI adds governance and maintenance responsibilities

A 2026 African AI/evaluation study reports connectivity, training and language barriers in a qualitative sample. UNU's assessment distinguishes openness from legitimacy or safety. NetHope's analysis identifies uncertainty in funder–grantee expectations around oversight, data ownership and model use. These sources support treating AI adoption as a governance decision, not a default marker of modernity. [[19]](https://jmde.journals.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/jmde_1/article/download/1329/934) [[20]](https://unu.edu/publication/ai-systems-digital-public-goods) [[21]](https://nethope.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/AI_Governance_Funder_Grantee_Landscape_Analysis_FOR-PUBLIC.pdf)

HubForge should ask whether a tool improves a real task under local operating conditions. Relevant tests include low-bandwidth access, exportability, local-language error patterns, accessibility, verification effort and maintenance costs. Refusal and non-AI alternatives must remain legitimate choices. The protective response to a data risk may slow adoption, and that can be a desirable system response rather than a failure.

This research does not establish that open tools are always cheaper over their lifetime. The DPGA sustainability report was identified through a snippet only and should not be used to claim a financing result. Similarly, UNESCO's Tanzania consultation announcement establishes a process, not achieved readiness. [[22]](https://www.digitalpublicgoods.net/dpg-sustainability-report) [[23]](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-supports-consultations-ai-readiness-assessment-tanzania)

## Causal model and leverage hypotheses

A plus sign means that increasing one variable tends to increase the other, all else equal; a minus sign means the opposite. Neither sign means good or bad. Multiplying signs around a closed loop classifies its polarity: an even number of negative links is reinforcing, an odd number balancing. A reinforcing loop can sustain progress or a trap. A balancing loop can constrain growth or protect rights. The reviewed methodological article contains shorthand that should not replace this sign-product rule. [[7]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9661310/)

The proposed core mechanisms are capacity investment, community authority and evidence use, fair career access, reporting burden, workload and quality, and data-risk oversight. Every complete loop remains a HubForge hypothesis. A source may support one relationship without establishing its direction, strength or delay in a new setting. The interactive map should expose assumptions instead of concealing them behind a polished graphic.

Leverage should be judged against feasibility and authority. HubForge can change its own tools, support practices and transparency immediately. It can negotiate shared learning arrangements with partners. It cannot independently change public procurement or compel funders to finance overhead. Institutional interventions therefore require a coalition and an explicit decision owner.

A useful validation question is “What would we observe if this relationship were wrong?” If mentoring improves competence but does not improve access to paid work, investigate commissioning barriers rather than automatically expand mentoring. If dashboards increase reporting speed but not decision use, revisit incentives and meeting routines. If broader data access reduces trust, strengthen rights and protection rather than maximise collection.

## Proposed 2027–2036 portfolio

| Period | Proposed emphasis | Decision gate, not a promised target |
|---|---|---|
| 2027–2029 | Validate with plural stakeholders; establish baselines; run a few bounded learning pilots using existing offers | Continue only where need, capacity, safeguards and a decision owner are established |
| 2030–2032 | Adapt and replicate mechanisms that show credible usefulness; strengthen university/VOPE/funder collaboration | Scale only with retained local capability, fair participation and sustainable operating support |
| 2033–2036 | Seek institutional ownership, durable financing and interoperable shared infrastructure | Transfer, sustain or retire offers according to evidence, not organisational attachment |

The first portfolio should connect existing work rather than add a new platform for every barrier. Playbooks can support practical methods; advisory can support decision-focused assignments; Eval Corps can support scoped contributions and learning; platforms can reduce recurring task friction. These are proposed contribution pathways, not measured impact claims.

Track decision changes, community influence, paid career progression, protected learning time, accessible sustained use and maintenance capacity. Pair each with a counter-indicator: burden, unpaid labour, exclusion, harm, dependency or abandonment. Downloads and attendance can describe reach but should not stand in for these outcomes.

Review assumptions at least annually and when a significant funding, regulatory or technology change occurs. Test contrasting scenarios: tighter philanthropic budgets, greater AI adoption with weak safeguards, and stronger local commissioning. The aim is to choose work that remains useful across conditions, not forecast which scenario will occur. Anticipatory-governance guidance offers a relevant further method resource, but its full recommendations were not assessed here. [[24]](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/05/towards-anticipatory-governance-guidelines-for-public-sector-organisations_c1a40090/a5203d0b-en.pdf)

## Limitations and unresolved evidence

This is secondary research, not stakeholder consultation. No HubForge-specific causal effects, budgets, delivery capacities or partnership commitments were independently established. Much evidence is contextual or normative. Older methodological sources remain useful, but 2021–2024 institutional accounts should not be treated as a 2026 baseline. Several sources were reviewed only as saved search excerpts; their use is deliberately restricted.

The source set also identifies comparative Latin American/African evaluation-use research and a Sub-Saharan Africa/South Asia research agenda. Only their scope was established, so country-level conclusions require further reading. [[25]](https://www.deval.org/fileadmin/Redaktion/PDF/05-Publikationen/Externe_Publikationen/2023_ECD/_Spanish__Limited_use_of_evaluative_evidence_in_public_policy_Policy_Brieffinal-esp-0423.pdf) [[26]](https://www.grtd.fcdo.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/RCC_EIPM_pathfinding_paper.pdf) Contemporary ALNAP search evidence points toward institutional power, but was not used to claim intervention effectiveness. [[27]](https://alnap.hacdn.io/media/documents/Ch4.0Summary_SOHS2026_Report_091026.pdf)

India's official CSR FAQ is jurisdiction-specific and partly stale: it retains an expense ceiling superseded by a later amendment. Do not use this report as legal or current compliance advice; obtain current operative rules before designing a CSR-funded engagement. [[28]](https://www.csr.gov.in/content/csr/global/master/home/helpandfaqs.html) Full-cost funding needs cannot be inferred from that regime or generalised across countries.

## Recommendations

Adopt the map as a working inquiry tool, not an organisational truth statement. Assemble a compensated reference group that includes communities and frontline workers alongside institutions with money and authority. Ask participants to challenge separate maps before negotiating a combined one; apparent consensus can hide unequal freedom to disagree.

Select a small number of mechanisms for initial testing. For each, name the decision owner, target group, contribution HubForge can realistically make, evidence that would weaken the hypothesis, safeguards and a stop/scale decision. Record disagreement and unintended effects, not only successful stories.

Before launching another initiative, ask whether an existing VOPE, university, public body or local organisation is better placed to lead. HubForge can contribute infrastructure or facilitation without owning the programme. Publish a proportionate learning account explaining what changed, what did not and what remains uncertain.
